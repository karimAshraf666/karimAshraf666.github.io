Playful / Founder, product manager & designer / 2026

A world for game nights, city days and things worth keeping.

Playful is where I take a product beyond the design file. I shape the experience, direct AI-assisted development, inspect the working software and build the tools needed to run it.

My role
Founder, product manager & product designer
Stage
Self-initiated, ongoing. Public experiences with beta and experimental areas.
Responsibility
Product direction, UX, visual design, architecture, AI-assisted delivery and QA
Core stack
Next.js, React, TypeScript, Supabase / Postgres, MapLibre, Vercel and GitHub

01 / The idea

Make it easier to find something you actually want to do.

Playful grew from the things I enjoy: games with friends, exploring Cairo, making things and caring for animals. The product brings those interests together without making every experience behave like the same app.

Product management

Choose what to build and what to hold back.

I define the purpose, first useful scope, constraints and release checks. A feature in the codebase is not automatically ready for customers.

Product design

Give each experience its own interaction model.

A player needs a fast join flow. A workshop needs privacy. A day out needs a place and a plan. Those differences shape the screen, not just its colour.

Visual direction

A handmade world with practical controls.

Paper textures, illustrations, game covers and warm colours create a recognizable family. Clear labels and readable panels keep the artwork from becoming the instructions.

AI-assisted building

I direct the work and own the review.

Claude and ChatGPT help implement and explore. I remain responsible for the brief, product decisions, integration and acceptance of what ships.

02 / Play together

One shared screen. Everyone's phone. Different ways to play.

Party puts hosting and joining next to each other. Game cards show the player range, time and premise before someone opens a room. The current lineup includes word association, memory, social dilemmas, a rescue relay and stealth games, with past experiments kept separate.

Select any project screen to inspect it at full size.

Experience decision

The television is a stage, not a form.

The display carries progress and reveals. Phones carry private answers and controls. Joining by code avoids turning a casual game night into an account-setup task.

Technical decision

Rules live on the server, not in each player's browser.

Room identity, game state, permissions and separate host/player views support different formats. Reconnection and recovery matter as much as the first successful join.

03 / Work together

A workshop should leave the room with something useful.

Party for Teams extends the room system into four working formats: Warm Start, Idea Rally, Team Reset and Room Vote. Each is organized around a job and a takeaway, rather than a list of workshop features.

Participation

Private input before the reveal.

People contribute without performing for the room. Passing is allowed. The facilitator can pace the session but cannot inspect someone else's unfinished answer.

Decision quality

A vote and a decision are different things.

The interface records the room's signal separately from the named decision-maker's call. An experiment card is not final until its required fields are complete.

Privacy in the implementation

Hidden means absent from the response.

Server-side views only send permitted fields to the wall and each phone. Drafts and ballots are not merely hidden with CSS. End-to-end checks inspect response data for leaks.

Reliable interaction

A repeated tap must not count twice.

Commands carry request IDs and the phase version they came from. Retries do not duplicate an action, and a stale command cannot change the next stage.

04 / Head outdoors

Turn a vague wish to go out into a place and a plan.

Outdoors connects Ventures, Solo and Paws. Ventures brings together Cairo places and events, while Solo starts from the hesitation behind going alone. Paws adds pet-care discovery with direct provider contact rather than a booking promise.

I separate original suggestions from externally sourced listings, and illustrations from photographs. A recommendation can be warm and personal without pretending to be verified information about a venue.

05 / Find and keep

A shared identity without forcing a shared template.

The profile brings together a character, nickname, private picks, optional artwork journeys and a separate ownership shelf. Its read model resolves identity from the signed-in session, not a user ID supplied by the browser.

Shared search matches real catalogue records and routes people to the relevant product. It does not pretend a basic name-and-place search understands every mood. More specific discovery belongs inside the experience designed for it.

06 / Under the hood

The experience reaches into the system behind it.

A Next.js and TypeScript application connects the products to Supabase authentication and Postgres. Domain-specific data sits alongside shared identity, media, permissions and operating controls. Vercel and GitHub support delivery.

Data and access

One person, with separate permissions.

Magic-link authentication supports a common profile. Customer identity does not grant operator access. Orders, access grants and personal artwork remain different records with different rules.

Rooms and sessions

Reuse foundations, not every interaction.

Teams reuses Party's codes, tokens, presence and polling transport. Its own state machine controls collection, reveal and takeaway. Infrastructure is shared, but the purpose is different.

Founder console

The product needs a way to be operated.

A separate admin surface covers catalogue and media work, orders and access, game content, provider information, feedback and runtime status. Public availability is controlled, not hardcoded into a marketing page.

Experimental builds

Keep ambitious work behind the right gate.

The codebase also includes Three.js game-world explorations and a separate Cloudflare realtime layer. Their existence is not a claim that every experiment is publicly ready or enabled.

07 / How I build

AI helps me try more. The useful part is deciding what to keep.

I work in small, complete flows: define the job and constraints, describe the states, direct the implementation, inspect the browser and compare it with the source. When the result changes, the specifications and checks need to change too.

Product scope

A polished screen is not a release decision.

Checkout, bookings and experimental games need their own readiness checks. Leaving a capability closed can be right even when its UI exists.

Design quality

Personality has to survive real use.

I review artwork alongside contrast, touch controls, screen fit, responsive layouts and reduced-motion behaviour. The visual world should support the task.

Verification

Test the rule, not just the screenshot.

A privacy promise needs a response-data check. A workshop needs a valid takeaway. A controller needs recovery when its connection drops. Those are design acceptance criteria.

Video-assisted review

Watch the moments between screens.

I use a video analyzer in my building workflow to flag mid-flow failures and layout changes a still image misses. I confirm its findings in the product before making changes.
