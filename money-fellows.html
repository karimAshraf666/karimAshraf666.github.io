The business

The product is a circle, and the slot you take decides what it costs you.

A money circle is the digital version of the Egyptian gam’eya. A group commits to paying in each month for a fixed run, and each month one member receives the whole pot. Everyone contributes the same total. The only thing that differs is when your turn arrives, and that single variable is the entire commercial and risk model of the product.

  • Why the join is the business event

    A circle only runs if it fills, and it only pays out if every member keeps paying. Signing up is not the commercial moment. Joining a circle and staying in it is, which is why the funnel I worked on ends at circle entry rather than at registration.

  • Why the match has to be honest

    Putting someone in a circle they cannot sustain is worse for the business than not filling the circle. A recommendation that flatters the customer creates a default later, so relevance here is a risk control as much as a growth lever.

  • Why clarity is a regulated requirement

    Early slots are credit. That makes eligibility, limits and the language around them a compliance surface, not a copy exercise. The interface has to state a decision a customer may not want to hear, and still leave them able to act.

The product question

How do you move a person from “I want this” to “I know my next step”?

Money Fellows makes the familiar Egyptian savings circle more accessible, predictable and actionable. My work sat where an aspiration has to become an informed action, without hiding the requirements, the consequences, or the social nature of joining a circle. The task was rarely to make finance look simpler. It was to make the path ahead genuinely easier to act on.

Where I focused

Four surfaces, one throughline.

These are separate product areas with separate teams and separate risks, but they share a single job: turn a moment of intent into a next step a person can actually take and trust.

01 / Goals

Make an aspiration actionable.

Translate a future facing intention into a path a person can read: what they are working toward, what has to happen next, and how a savings circle helps close the gap.

02 / Recommendations

Earn the next action through relevance.

Design entry points and bundles so the next circle reads as contextually useful for this person and this goal, rather than as a generic product prompt.

03 / Onboarding

Build confidence before commitment.

Explain an unfamiliar financial model without slowing down the people who are already ready to move. Language, sequence, and interface states all carry weight here.

04 / Migration

Protect clarity at speed.

Turn a moving, time critical product change into a coherent journey across state design, messaging, edge cases and handoff, so people were carried through rather than dropped.

Goals V2

A goal is not a dashboard metric. It is a promise a person is trying to keep.

Goals V2 pulled a scattered set of money terms, status states and progress treatments into one system a person could learn once and reuse everywhere. It settled the interface on four clear financial concepts, Goal, Received, Scheduled and Short or Extra, and a single coverage model applied identically across Home, My Goals and Goal Details. Eligibility, limits and credit communication

Turning risk-sensitive rules into something a person can act on.

Behind every slot a customer can see sits a decision they cannot: whether they qualify, for how much, and on what terms. Those answers come from risk models and regulatory requirements, and they arrive as constraints rather than as suggestions. My job was to give them a shape a customer can understand and respond to, without softening what they actually mean.

  • Eligibility and limits

    A customer meets a number that was decided about them. The design question is what a limit is allowed to imply, what it must never imply, and what the person can do next when the answer is smaller than they hoped.

  • KYC

    Verification arrives at the least convenient moment, because that is when it is required. The work is sequencing it so it reads as the product taking the customer seriously rather than as an obstacle appearing without warning.

  • Credit communication

    Saying no, or saying less than requested, is a design surface with legal weight. It has to be accurate, consistent everywhere it appears, and specific enough that the customer knows whether anything can change.

  • Lifecycle changes

    People do not stay in one state. Terms change, statuses change, circles end. Each transition needs messaging that assumes the customer remembers the previous state, because they do.

This is the part of the work my engineering background actually earns its keep on. Reading the rule as a system, arguing the edge cases with product, engineering and risk before anything is drawn, and knowing which constraints are genuinely fixed and which are only habit.

Onboarding

Teach an unfamiliar model without slowing down the ready.

Onboarding had to explain how a digital savings circle works to people meeting it for the first time, while staying out of the way of people who already understood it and wanted to move. Language, sequence and interface state did most of that work.

  • Explain in the flow, not before it. The model is taught at the point a person needs it, rather than in a wall of introduction they will skip.
  • One idea per screen. Each step carries a single decision, so the sequence stays legible and reversible.
  • A confident exit. Completion is designed to feel like a commitment kept, which is what makes the next goal easier to start.

Research and experiments

Turn learning into a route people recognise.

The onboarding experience was shaped by insights from 70+ Money Fellows interviews, then continuously tested and refined rather than shipped once. Across the flow this reached roughly 99% screen to screen conversion, a sign that people could find their way through it rather than merely being exposed to it. Every surface here was treated the same way: listen, frame a hypothesis, prototype it, and let the next round of evidence correct the last.

Cross-functional delivery

Design the system behind the screen, then stay with it to production.

  1. 01 Map the rule before the screen. Agree the states, the edge cases and what each one is permitted to say, with product, engineering and risk in the room.
  2. 02 Prototype so the trade-offs are felt early, and fix the language once so it holds across every surface a customer can reach it from.
  3. 03 Write the specification the build is actually made from, including the states nobody wants to think about.
  4. 04 Stay through implementation review and QA, because the distance between an approved design and production is where clarity quietly goes missing.
