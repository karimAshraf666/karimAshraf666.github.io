# Brand Art Direction

Turn a client-specific visual idea into a small set of behaviours that survives new
content, formats and makers. This is a method for design judgment, not a Paula Scher
style preset.

Adapted from [Paula Scher's Hatch talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UsXYxezOUMU).
The supplied source is auto-generated captions. Timestamped examples and the limits of
that evidence are in [source notes](references/source-notes.md); read them when explaining
the method's provenance or relying on a particular example.

## Fit into the existing work

- Inspect the client's current work, assets, `DESIGN.md` and DNA before proposing a look.
  An existing client system outranks this method's suggestions. Distinguish a requested
  redesign from an application of an existing identity.
- `website-craft` owns the full website workflow. Use this skill inside it only when the
  identity idea or its extension needs work. `agent-ready-design-system` owns tokens,
  components and implementation contracts. `design-dna` codifies an approved visual winner;
  this skill helps find and test that winner, without making a second DNA record.
- Keep work within the requested deliverable. A direction brief does not authorize image
  generation, custom font production, new tools, spending, or changes to a live brand.

## Find the idea, then demonstrate its behaviour

**Ground the direction.** Write the intended recognition or feeling in one sentence using
the client's actual content and audience. Separate observed facts, user decisions and
your assumptions. Ask only for consequential missing information; never invent a client
interview, audience finding or approval.

**Identify what does the visual work.** Choose a leading relationship between type, image,
marks, scale and colour. Explain why it belongs to this subject. A ticket becoming a B was
Scher's Berkeley Rep solution; the reusable lesson is to derive a recognisable operation
from the subject, not to reuse tickets or letter tricks for every client. [3:07-4:44]

**Judge scale and colour in context.** Show the type beside real content, imagery and the
place it must live, rather than presenting an isolated logo as the whole identity.
Compare actual display/body hierarchy, density, margins and colour coverage. Use measured
client references or visible trials to set relationships; the talk supplies no universal
ratio, palette or size. Test the formats that place the most different demands on it.
Scher describes scale and colour as transferable across technology and shows museum type
against architectural drawings. [1:20-1:50; 5:39-6:24]

**Make variation bounded and useful.** Keep the identity-bearing decisions fixed and name
the choices another maker can change: for example, approved width, alignment, image crop
or layout. Demonstrate those choices with real short/long content and contrasting formats.
Show a boundary example where the identity breaks. If makers keep drifting, inspect the
instructions and examples before adding more stylistic rules. Berkeley Rep received
usable layouts; MAM received type widths and alignment options after overly free use had
to be pulled back. These are examples of bounded choice, not universal prescriptions for
one font or stretched letters. [3:54-5:24; 6:42-8:22]

**Protect the source of distinctiveness.** For a graphic or illustration system, start
from the client's real visual material or a deliberately authored seed: marks, shapes,
texture or lettering. State what must survive transformation, then compare outputs with
that seed. Reject polish that erases the defining behaviour. Handmade work and generative
tools are options according to the brief; neither is a required aesthetic. For
performance.gov, Scher describes returning to rough painted inputs after a cleaner
generated direction felt generic. [15:31-17:46]

**Test communication as part of the identity.** Use realistic titles and paragraphs, not
placeholder copy. Where dense material defeats the design, define a readable content
container and edit for the user's understanding while preserving meaning. Expose missing
facts or required qualifications instead of deleting them to fit. This adapts the talk's
editorial templates and shortened stories; it is not authority to rewrite unrequested
material. [17:54-20:30]

## Review and handoff

Render the direction at its intended sizes; use `design-loop` for visual comparison and
independent critique. Judge the pixels: recognisability across examples, hierarchy at a
glance, legibility, and whether allowed variation remains coherent. If only a written
direction was requested, label it a proposal and leave visual validation open.

Return a compact direction record in the project's existing design source: the idea and
why it fits; fixed decisions and permitted variation; examples and one failure boundary;
what was actually inspected; unresolved decisions. Explain choices through communication
and use, not an appeal to Scher or Pentagram. Record the human's approval accurately. Once
the look is approved, offer `design-dna` to codify it.

Never default to oversized condensed type, chaotic layouts, custom fonts, painterly marks
or museum branding merely because they appear in Scher's work. Transfer the reasoning;
the client's identity supplies the appearance.
