Sharwa / Product Design / Aug 2022 to Sep 2023

An unfamiliar way
to shop, made
ordinary.

Sharwa let people across MENA buy everyday products together at a lower price. The hard part was never the discount. It was helping shoppers who were new to buying in an app understand what a group is, why it saves them money, and what they are expected to do next.

Role
Product Designer
Period
Aug 2022 to Sep 2023
Domain
Social commerce, group buying, MENA
Surfaces
Arabic mobile app and responsive web app

The problem

The saving was easy to explain.
The behaviour was not.

Group buying asks a shopper to do several unfamiliar things at once, and much of Sharwa's audience was not especially tech savvy. For someone whose mental model is a normal cart and a normal checkout, every one of these is a reason to close the app.

  1. 01

    Commit before you know the outcome

    You choose the group price before the group is complete, so you are agreeing to something that has not happened yet.

  2. 02

    Depend on other people

    The price only holds if others join. That makes the purchase social, and it puts part of the result outside your control.

  3. 03

    Act inside a time limit

    A group expires. Urgency helps conversion, but it also raises the cost of not understanding the mechanic.

The work / 01

Put a live group on the home screen.

The home revamp had to answer "what is a group order?" without a tutorial nobody reads. So the open group became a card you meet immediately, and it carries the whole mechanic in one glance.

  • Who is already in. A stacked row of member avatars with a remaining count, so the group is visibly a group.
  • What you save. The saving is stated as an amount in pounds rather than a percentage to decode.
  • How long is left. A live countdown in hours and minutes gives the decision a shape.
  • One action. A single join button. No configuration and no second screen before the commitment is made.

Seeing other people mid purchase does more persuading than any explanation of the model. The category grid and the promotional rail sit underneath, so a shopper who does not want a group can still shop normally.

The work / 02

Turn a social mechanic into a numbered task.

The group order is the moment the product either makes sense or loses the customer. Rather than describe it, the flow was built as a three step progression a shopper could sit inside: add, confirm, share.

  • A labelled stepper. Each step is named and the current one is marked, so there is always an answer to "where am I and what is left".
  • A running total. The basket value stays pinned in the header through every step of the flow.
  • A countdown that arrives with the commitment. The timer appears once there is something to lose, not before.
  • An explainer at the point of need. The share step spells out the three things that happen next: share the link, your group adds items, the group completes.

Sharing is where a group order succeeds or dies, so it got the most direct treatment on the screen: a plain sequence, a large share action, and the channels people actually use.

The work / 03

Price the new behaviour against the familiar one.

The clearest thing the product could do was stop explaining group buying and start showing it as a comparison. On the product page the two options sit next to each other at the point of decision: buy on your own at one price, or buy with friends at a lower one.

  • Two prices, one row. The familiar option stays visible. The new behaviour has to win on its merits rather than by being the only door.
  • Joinable groups underneath. Groups already running on that product, so joining is often faster than starting one.
  • Cumulative saving in the account. A signed-in shopper sees what they have saved with Sharwa to date, which turns a one-off tactic into a reason to return.
  • A guest path that does not dead end. Browsing works before signing up, with the account prompt placed where the benefit is concrete.

I also led the planning and design of the responsive web application, and ran alignment workshops across Product, Operations and Marketing so the mechanic was described the same way everywhere a customer could meet it.

What I took from it

A new behaviour is learned from other people, not from copy.

The changes that moved this product were rarely explanations. They were making a live group visible, giving the flow numbered steps, and letting the new option stand beside the familiar one so a shopper could judge it themselves. That instinct has stayed with me on every product since.
