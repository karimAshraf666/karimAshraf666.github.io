Bluworks / Product Design / Dec 2023 to Jun 2024

One HR platform,
two very different
places to use it.

Bluworks manages scheduling, attendance, leave, loans and payroll for companies whose rules are all slightly different. My work ran along two tracks: making the web application configurable enough to fit those companies, and designing a separate fixed-device experience for the floor, where the office product could not go.

The shape of the work

Two products, not one feature set.

It would be easy to describe Bluworks as a single HR system I added modules to. It was not. The two tracks below have different users, different physical settings, different sessions and different failure modes, and treating them as one product is how you get an office tool bolted onto a factory wall.

Track A

The configurable HR web application

Used by HR staff, branch and area managers at a desk, signed in as themselves, working across an entire company. The design problem is variation: every client structures loans, approvals, holidays and payroll differently, so the product has to be shaped by configuration rather than by a new release for each customer.

  • Who HR, operations and branch management
  • Where A private screen, a long session
  • Risk Rules that do not fit the client

Track B

The fixed-device experience

A tablet mounted in a shared operational space, used for seconds at a time by many workers in sequence, with no assumption of a personal phone. I designed this in response to client safety and operational concerns about the existing approach, keeping the app functionality that still made sense in that setting and dropping what did not.

  • Who Frontline workers, in turn
  • Where A shared device, seconds per person
  • Risk One worker seeing another's data

Why the split matters

The same task changes completely when the device is shared.

Track A / 01

Start from the question, not the navigation.

An HR platform accumulates modules, and a sidebar of eleven items tells a manager nothing about where to begin. The landing view asks what they are here to review and answers with named jobs rather than product areas, each with a plain sentence describing what it does.

  • Jobs, not features. Schedule weekly shifts, track attendance, approve leave, manage salary calculations. The label is the task.
  • The sidebar still exists. People who know exactly where they are going are not slowed down by the friendlier route.
  • One vocabulary. The same names are used in the sidebar, the landing cards and the module headers, so nothing has to be re-learned.

Track A / 02

Configure it once. Operate it every day.

This pairing is the whole argument for a configurable product, and Loan Management is the clearest example of it. One screen is where a company encodes its own policy. The other is where that policy shows up as a manager's daily work. They are two tabs of the same module, and they have to be designed as a pair.

Track B

A screen on the wall is not a small desktop.

The second track began as a client problem rather than a feature request. Two things made the existing approach unworkable in some customer settings: clients did not want attendance running through personal phones, and plenty of those phones were not reliable enough to carry it anyway. The answer was a fixed tablet: one device, mounted in place, used briefly by many workers in turn, with a fingerprint or a face standing in for a login.

That changes the brief completely. There is no long session to return to and no privacy by default. The design has to get a worker in and out in seconds, show only what belongs to the person currently in front of it, and end cleanly so the next person starts from nothing. I kept the app functionality that still made sense in a shared setting and removed what only made sense on a personal device.

The work reopened sales conversations that the previous approach had closed, which is the outcome I would point to. I am not going to attach a number to it that I cannot evidence.

  • One task per visitThe device exists for a single moment in a worker's day, not for browsing.
  • No residueNothing personal survives into the next person's turn.
  • Legible at arm's lengthType and targets sized for a mounted screen, not a held one.
  • Works without a phoneNo assumption that every worker carries a device or an account on it.

Research for this track is published in the showcase: 15 short surveys and 15 one-to-one interviews across front-line staff and HR managers, plus a day of observation at each of two branches. In that group, 60% said they sometimes forget to clock in or out, 40% reported queueing at shift change, and 40% raised colleagues clocking in for each other. It is a small sample and I report it as one.

Track B / The shipped interface

The fixed tablet, screen by screen.

This is the Track B product, published in my public UXcel showcase. It is a separate application from the web platform above: Arabic, portrait, mounted in place, and built around biometric clock-in with a manual fallback. The interface reads right to left.

Identify, then authenticate

The device stays idle until a worker names themselves. Only once a person is chosen does the screen commit to a shift and switch the biometric options on, so nobody authenticates into somebody else's day.

Telling someone it worked, or did not

On a shared device the result has to be unmistakable from a step away, because the next person is already waiting. Success and failure both resolve in the same strip at the foot of the screen.

Manager mode, behind its own door

The same tablet carries a manager view, reached through a separate credential so a worker cannot wander into it. It answers the questions a floor manager asks at a glance, then gets out of the way.

Enrolment is the quiet half of biometrics

A biometric system is only as good as what it was given to match against. These screens make the gaps visible, so a manager can see who is missing a face or a fingerprint before it becomes an argument at shift change.

What I took from it

Configurability is a design problem before it is an engineering one.

Every client wanted the product to behave slightly differently, and the temptation is to answer that with settings. The useful version is deciding which differences are real, giving them a shape a customer can reason about, and making sure the daily screen still reads as one clear position rather than the sum of everything that was configured.
